What It Is — How Roof Tarping Works

Tarping is fast, but it has to be done right — a loose tarp in a storm is worse than no tarp. Here is the process.

  1. Roof assessed from the ground firstThe crew inspects from the ground and, only if the structure is sound, from a ladder. Nobody walks a fire-weakened roof.
  2. Damaged area is measuredEvery vent cut, hole, and missing-shingle section is measured so the tarp covers past the damage on all sides.
  3. Heavy-duty tarp is anchoredThe tarp is laid over the ridge where possible and anchored with battens and fasteners — not just weighted down.
  4. Edges are sealed against windEdges are tucked and sealed so wind cannot lift the tarp and drive rain underneath it.
  5. Photos go to your insurerBefore and after photos document the emergency mitigation for your claim.

When You Need It

If your roof took fire or firefighting damage, tarping is urgent — rain does not wait for repairs.

  1. Vents cut by firefightersCrews cut roof vents to release heat and smoke. Each cut is an open hole in your roof.
  2. Missing or charred shinglesFire and heat destroy shingles and underlayment, leaving the deck exposed.
  3. Visible holes or sagging deckingA sagging roof section means the structure underneath is compromised and water is already getting in.
  4. Water dripping into the atticAny drip after a fire means the roof envelope is open. The next rain will make it worse.
  5. Rain in the forecastEven a small storm can soak insulation, ceilings, and framing through a damaged roof.
  6. Ceiling stains spreadingStains that grow between rains are roof water finding its way down through your home.

What It Costs

Roof tarping typically costs $500–$1,500 nationally. Published rates put small tarps at $500–$1,500 and large or steep roofs at $1,500–$4,000 (DISS Restoration). Severely damaged roofs in major metro markets have been reported at $3,500–$9,000+.

What drives the price up or down:

  • Tarp size — a single vent cut costs far less than a full roof section.
  • Roof pitch — steep roofs take longer and need more anchoring.
  • Height and access — multi-story homes cost more to work on safely.
  • Storm conditions — emergency tarping during active weather is slower and riskier.
  • Structural damage — if the deck cannot be walked on, the crew works from ladders and lifts.

Quick Answers

How long does a roof tarp last?

A properly anchored heavy-duty tarp typically holds for weeks to a few months, but it is temporary protection, not a repair. It should be inspected after every major storm and replaced if it loosens or tears.

Does insurance cover roof tarping after a fire?

Emergency tarping is usually covered as mitigation under a standard homeowner policy, because it prevents further water damage. Keep the receipt and the before-and-after photos — we provide both for your claim.

Can a tarp be installed in the rain?

Yes, crews work in light rain when the roof is safe to access, because stopping water is the whole point. In severe weather or lightning, crews wait for a safe window and return as soon as conditions allow.

What if the roof is too damaged to walk on?

Crews do not walk fire-weakened roofs. They work from ladders, lifts, and the ground, anchoring the tarp from the edges. Safety comes first — a collapsed roof helps no one.

Is tarping enough, or do I need a new roof?

Tarping buys time. Whether the roof can be repaired or must be replaced depends on how much decking and structure the fire damaged — that is decided during the restoration assessment, not on the emergency visit.
