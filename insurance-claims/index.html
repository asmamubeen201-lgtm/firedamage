First 48 Hours — and Your Duty to Mitigate

After the fire department clears your home, the clock starts. Most policies require you to prevent further damage — this is called your duty to mitigate — and the steps are simple:

  1. Call your insurer within 24–48 hours.Most policies require prompt notice. Waiting can complicate or reduce your claim.
  2. Board up and tarp immediately.Broken windows and a damaged roof let in rain, animals, and intruders. Emergency board-up ($300–$900) and roof tarping ($500–$1,500) are mitigation, not optional extras.
  3. Do not throw anything away yet.Damaged items are evidence. Bag them, label them, and wait for the adjuster or your restoration pro to inventory them.
  4. Do not re-enter until it is safe.If the fire department has not cleared the structure, stay out. Soot and smoke residue are hazardous to breathe.
  5. Call a restoration pro the same day.Mitigation started in the first hours is cheaper than damage that spreads for a week.

Documenting the Damage

Your claim is only as strong as your documentation. Before any cleanup begins:

  • Photograph and video every room — walls, ceilings, floors, contents — before anything is moved or cleaned.
  • Make a list of damaged contents, room by room. Include approximate age and value where you can.
  • Keep the fire department report and any incident number. Ask the fire crew for it before they leave if you can.
  • Save every receipt: board-up, tarping, hotel, meals, storage. These are claim evidence.
  • Ask your restoration pro for a written scope of work and written documentation — cause, location, and scope — for the claim file.

ALE — Temporary Housing and Meals

ALE stands for Additional Living Expenses. If your home is uninhabitable, most homeowner policies reimburse the extra cost of living elsewhere: a hotel or rental, and meals above what you would normally spend at home. Keep every receipt for emergency board-up, temporary housing, and meals — ALE coverage usually reimburses these, but only what you can prove. Ask your adjuster what your ALE limit is before you book anything long-term.

You have the right to choose your own restoration contractor. In Texas, your insurer cannot force you to use their preferred vendor. What NOT to Sign

Two documents deserve a pause before your pen touches them:

Your full claim check.

Do not sign over your entire claim check to a contractor. Payment should follow a written scope and completed milestones — not a blank handover.

Assignment of Benefits (AoB).

An AoB hands your claim rights to someone else. Do not sign one — or any contract you do not fully understand — without getting the scope in writing first and asking your insurer or your state's Department of Insurance what it means for you.

Quick Answers — Fire Damage Insurance Claims

Will my homeowner's insurance cover fire damage?

Most standard HO-3 homeowner policies cover sudden and accidental fire damage, including smoke damage and water damage from firefighting. Policies often exclude gradual damage or damage you knew about and ignored. Act fast, document everything, and contact your insurer before remediation begins.

How fast do I need to notify my insurer after a fire?

Most policies require prompt notice — call your insurer within 24 to 48 hours of the fire. Waiting can complicate or reduce your claim. Call your insurer first, then call a restoration pro to start emergency mitigation.

Can I choose my own fire damage restoration contractor?

Yes. In Texas you have the right to choose your own restoration contractor — your insurer cannot force you to use their preferred vendor. Rules vary by state, so verify with your state's Department of Insurance, but never let an adjuster pick your contractor for you.

What is ALE coverage after a house fire?

ALE — Additional Living Expenses — covers temporary housing, hotel stays, and meals while your home is uninhabitable. Keep every receipt for emergency board-up, temporary housing, and meals; ALE coverage usually reimburses these costs.

Should I sign an Assignment of Benefits after a fire?

Do not sign an Assignment of Benefits — or sign over your full claim check — without understanding exactly what it gives away. Get the scope of work in writing first, and ask your insurer or your state's Department of Insurance before signing anything.

Does renters insurance cover fire damage?

A standard renters policy (HO-4) usually covers your personal belongings damaged by fire — furniture, clothes, electronics — and may include loss-of-use help if the rental is uninhabitable. The building itself is your landlord's policy, not yours. Document your belongings, notify your insurer promptly, and check your policy's limits and deductible — coverage varies, so verify the details with your insurer.
