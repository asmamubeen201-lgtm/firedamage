When You Need It

Residue is often invisible. These are the signs it is there.

  1. Surfaces feel greasy or stickyA slick film on walls, glass, or metal after a fire is wet or protein residue.
  2. The smell returns in heat or humidityOdor that fades in cool weather and returns on warm days is residue releasing from porous materials.
  3. Residue on glass and mirrorsA hazy film on glass that will not wipe clean is smoke residue, not dust.
  4. Yellowing on light-colored surfacesProtein smoke yellows paint and plastics even when there is no visible soot.
  5. Streaks when you wipeIf a damp cloth leaves streaks instead of cleaning, the residue needs professional chemistry.
  6. Contents smell after washingClothes, drapes, and upholstery that still smell after normal washing carry residue in the fibers.

Quick Answers

What is protein smoke, and why is it so hard to remove?

Protein smoke comes from burning food, grease, and other organic material — most often kitchen fires. It leaves an invisible, sticky residue that standard cleaners cannot lift and a smell that is far stronger than its appearance suggests.

Why does the smoke smell come back after I clean?

Because the residue is still in porous materials — paint, drywall, wood, and fabric. It releases odor when the home warms up or humidity rises. Full removal means cleaning with the right chemistry plus deodorization and sealing.

Can smoke residue damage electronics?

Yes. Soot and smoke residue are acidic and can corrode circuit boards and contacts over time. Electronics that were exposed should be professionally cleaned and powered off until they are.

How long does residue removal take?

Most homes take 1–3 days. A single room can be done in a day; a whole house with heavy protein residue takes longer.

Will my walls need repainting after residue removal?

Often, yes — but only after cleaning and sealing. A smoke-sealing primer goes on first so residue cannot bleed through the new paint. Painting without that step is how stains and smells come back.
