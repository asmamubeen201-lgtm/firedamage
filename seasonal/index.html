❄️ Winter — Heating Fires

The risk

Winter cold snaps bring heating-equipment and space-heater fires. Furnaces that sat idle all year fire up under load, and portable space heaters run for hours near bedding, curtains, and furniture. In Texas, winter cold snaps are the season's defining fire risk — and in the Panhandle and North Texas, hard winters make heating fires a recurring problem.

How to prevent it

  • Keep space heaters at least 3 feet from anything that can burn, and never leave one running unattended or overnight.
  • Have your furnace serviced before the first cold snap — a cracked heat exchanger or clogged filter is a fire waiting to happen.
  • Test smoke detectors when the heating season starts, and replace batteries.
  • Know where your shutoff valve is before a pipe or heater fails — winter emergencies move fast.

🌧️ Spring — Lightning and Storms

The risk

Spring is severe-weather season, and lightning-strike fires are its signature. A single strike can ignite an attic or roof and smolder undetected for hours. In Texas, spring severe weather brings lightning fires across the state, and storm-driven electrical damage follows the same fronts. Hail and wind can also tear roofing open, leaving homes exposed to the next rain.

How to prevent it

  • After any nearby lightning strike, check the attic and roofline for the smell of smoke — attic fires can hide for hours.
  • Keep gutters clear and roof flashing intact so storm water does not find its way in.
  • Surge-protect major appliances and electronics against storm-driven electrical damage.
  • If a storm damages your roof, tarp it the same day — roof tarping runs $500–$1,500 for a small tarp and stops water damage before it starts.

☀️ Summer — Wildfire and Grill Fires

The risk

Summer is wildfire season. In West and Central Texas, dry years bring recurring wildfire risk to the urban fringe, and in California the January 2025 Palisades and Eaton fires showed how fast wildfire can reach entire neighborhoods. Embers travel far ahead of the flame front — a single ember in a gutter or vent can ignite a house. Backyard grills add a second summer risk: grease fires and grills placed too close to siding or decking.

How to prevent it

  • Clear gutters, dry leaves, and debris from rooflines and vents before the dry season — embers need fuel to catch.
  • Keep grills at least 10 feet from the house, deck railings, and overhanging branches. Never leave a lit grill unattended.
  • During wildfire smoke events, keep windows closed and change HVAC filters — smoke infiltration can damage a home even without flames.
  • If wildfire smoke has reached your home, wildfire smoke odor removal ($1,000–$4,000) covers HVAC, attic insulation, and soft contents.

🍂 Fall and Holidays — Cooking Fires

The risk

Fall is cooking-fire season, and it peaks on the biggest cooking day of the year. Kitchen fires are the #1 residential fire type — grease and appliance fires with heavy protein-smoke contamination that is invisible and brutally hard to remove. Thanksgiving brings the year's highest kitchen-fire risk: crowded kitchens, multiple burners, deep fryers, and distracted cooks. A contained kitchen fire typically runs $3,000–$8,000 to restore.

How to prevent it

  • Never leave cooking unattended — especially frying. If you must leave the kitchen, turn the burner off.
  • Keep a lid nearby for grease fires: slide it on, turn off the heat, and never use water on a grease fire.
  • Keep turkey fryers outdoors, away from the house and deck, on a flat surface.
  • Clean the oven and stovetop before the holiday rush — built-up grease is fuel.
